# Are our promotions paying off?

**Executive summary** · Vachirasak Narkklin · [Full analysis on GitHub](https://github.com/Vachirasak-Narkklin/promo-uplift-project)

## The question

Discounts make sales jump, but part of every jump comes from customers who would have bought anyway, at full price. Using two years of transactions from about 2,500 grocery households (Dunnhumby), I estimated how much volume promotions really add, whether current discount levels make money, and whether targeted coupon campaigns pay back.

## What I found

**Only about 18% of volume is genuinely driven by promotions.** The other 82% would have sold anyway. Some big categories hardly respond: bread is discounted around 20% on average, but only about 4% of its volume is extra.

**Most categories are discounted deeper than pays off.** At a 30% margin, a 15% discount needs twice the volume just to break even. Sales do rise with deeper discounts (soft drinks roughly double between 5% and 35% off), but not by enough. If the store funds discounts itself, 118 of 149 categories would earn more gross profit with a shallower discount, and none would earn more with a deeper one. Moving each to its best level would mean about 5% fewer units, 3% more revenue and around 40% more gross profit.

**Supplier funding decides whether a deep discount is worth it.** About 1 in 5 categories pay off at today's discount without help. About 2 in 5, including soft drinks, beef and milk, only pay off if suppliers fund 70% or more of the discount.

**Coupon campaigns work, but far less than the raw numbers suggest.** Targeted households spent $25.6 more per week than others, but the store had chosen heavier shoppers. The real effect is about **$2.2 per household per week**. After coupon and contact costs, that still returns about **$3.3 per targeted household**.

**We can't yet tell who responds best.** My targeting model couldn't separate strong and weak responders on new households, so I'm not recommending narrower targeting from it.

## What I'd recommend

1. **Use supplier funding as the decision rule.** For each category, the analysis gives the minimum supplier contribution that makes today's discount depth worthwhile. Bring those numbers to supplier negotiations, and scale back deep discounts where suppliers won't meet them.
2. **Start with the biggest gaps.** Soft drinks, milk, beef, frozen meals and bread show the largest profit difference between today's discount and the best level.
3. **Review loss-making promotions.** Chicken, pork, ice cream and hot dogs lose money at today's depth unless suppliers are funding them or they're deliberate traffic builders. Either should be a conscious choice.
4. **Keep the coupon campaigns, and add a random holdout.** Leave out 10–20% of the households we'd normally target in the next campaign. That gives clean data to learn who actually responds.

## How sure are we?

Product costs and supplier funding aren't in the data, so margins (25–45% by department) are assumptions. The findings hold across a range of margins and funding levels, but the exact profit numbers move. Discounts weren't randomised, and basket effects (shoppers buying other items on a deal trip) aren't counted. **Test any change in a handful of stores before rolling it out.**
